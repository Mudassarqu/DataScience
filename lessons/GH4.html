Design Brief

Context that frames the whole Criterion B response for this lesson.

Unit objective: Visualise health data to understand how heart rate, oxygen levels and body temperature reflect physical condition and prevent overexertion.

Problem context (community need): School sports days and community fitness events have no easy way to check a student's pulse and oxygen before exertion. A small clip or wand gives a quick, friendly check.

Design task: Design a physical, 3D-printable / laser-cut housing that holds the Arduino and integrates the Health sensor (heart rate, SpO₂, temperature), so a community can read and use the data.

User: school nurses, PE teachers and students.

Data Science Link

Your product exists to produce good data. This is how the unit's Four Big Ideas connect to the design.

What data does the sensor give? Measures heart rate (BPM), blood oxygen (SpO₂) and temperature in real time.

Low end: Normal ranges: balanced physical condition and readiness for activity.
High end: Elevated heart rate or temperature may signal fatigue, stress or overexertion; low oxygen can signal poor circulation.

How the data is useful: Detects early signs of stress, illness or exhaustion and supports smarter decisions about rest.

1 · CollectionThe sensor reads the world; the housing must not disturb it.
2 · OrganisationArduino sends readings to Python; clean errors, missing values and duplicates.
3 · AnalysisLine, bar and pie charts reveal patterns and compare places or times.
4 · InterpretationThe community decides what to do with the pattern.

Modelling Worksheet — Making the Physical Model

Follow in order. Lab tools: 3D printer (PETG), laser cutter, hex driver, 320-grit sandpaper, hot glue gun.

  1. Check the CAD: open the chosen STL in TinkerCAD or your slicer, set units to millimetres and confirm the envelope is 130.0 × 96.0 × 66.6 mm (limit 140 × 110 × 80 mm).
  2. Slice the printed parts (Controller box, Finger groove, Hinge block, Lower jaw, Upper jaw, Upper jaw LED window) in Cura/PrusaSlicer: PETG, 0.2 mm layers, 15–20% infill, supports only where an overhang needs them.
  3. Laser-cut the MDF parts (Base plate) from 3 mm MDF using the top orthographic view as the footprint.
  4. De-burr and sand every printed part (320 grit); check the sensor opening and screw holes so nothing binds or blocks the sensor.
  5. Fit the sensor: Seat the sensor window flush in the finger groove, with the top jaw's LED window directly over it, and cover the PCB edges with tape to block side light.
  6. Assemble following the numbered exploded view and the BOM: fasteners finger-tight first.
  7. Wire and test: connect the sensor to the Arduino, upload your test sketch (Criterion C), and watch the readings in the Serial Monitor.
  8. Install in the community location from your design brief and confirm the sensor sees what it should.
  9. Function-test against specifications 7.1 and 7.2: Reads pulse and SpO₂ within 20 seconds of a finger being placed, and shows both on a display. Record the results in your portfolio.
  10. Finish: spray or vinyl-wrap in the colour scheme from specification 1.1, then re-test.
