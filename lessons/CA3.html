Design Brief

Context that frames the whole Criterion B response for this lesson.

Unit objective: Visualise light intensity patterns to track daylight changes, reduce energy use and support climate-smart decisions.

Problem context (community need): School corridors and car parks across the UAE keep lights on all day because nobody measures daylight. A daylight tracker could switch lights off and shading on automatically, cutting electricity use.

Design task: Design a physical, 3D-printable / laser-cut housing that holds the Arduino and integrates the Light detector (LDR), so a community can read and use the data.

User: a school facilities team or a building manager.

Data Science Link

Your product exists to produce good data. This is how the unit's Four Big Ideas connect to the design.

What data does the sensor give? Measures light intensity using analog values typically scaled from 0 to 100.

Low end: Lower values: more light detected, so brighter conditions and clear weather.
High end: Higher values: less light detected, so dim conditions and cloudy weather.

How the data is useful: Lighting or shading can adjust automatically in dim conditions, reducing energy use.

1 · CollectionThe sensor reads the world; the housing must not disturb it.
2 · OrganisationArduino sends readings to Python; clean errors, missing values and duplicates.
3 · AnalysisLine, bar and pie charts reveal patterns and compare places or times.
4 · InterpretationThe community decides what to do with the pattern.

Solar pole with sky-facing light head

Modelling Worksheet — Making the Physical Model

Follow in order. Lab tools: 3D printer (PETG), laser cutter, hex driver, 320-grit sandpaper, hot glue gun.

  1. Check the CAD: open the chosen STL in TinkerCAD or your slicer, set units to millimetres and confirm the envelope is 130.0 × 110.0 × 189.8 mm (limit 140 × 120 × 200 mm).
  2. Slice the printed parts (Accent band, Base housing, Head arm, Pole) in Cura/PrusaSlicer: PETG, 0.2 mm layers, 15–20% infill, supports only where an overhang needs them.
  3. Laser-cut the MDF parts (Base plate) from 3 mm MDF using the top orthographic view as the footprint.
  4. De-burr and sand every printed part (320 grit); check the sensor opening and screw holes so nothing binds or blocks the sensor.
  5. Fit the sensor: Fix the LDR window facing straight up through the diffuser, keeping the pot accessible with a screwdriver but out of direct rain.
  6. Assemble following the numbered exploded view and the BOM: fasteners finger-tight first.
  7. Wire and test: connect the sensor to the Arduino, upload your test sketch (Criterion C), and watch the readings in the Serial Monitor.
  8. Install in the community location from your design brief and confirm the sensor sees what it should.
  9. Function-test against specifications 7.1 and 7.2: Reports light on a 0–100 scale and tells day from night with fewer than 2 wrong readings per day. Record the results in your portfolio.
  10. Finish: spray or vinyl-wrap in the colour scheme from specification 1.1, then re-test.
