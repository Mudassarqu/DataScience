Design Brief

Context that frames the whole Criterion B response for this lesson.

Unit objective: Visualise data to determine the best conditions for growing crops and understand how rain, irrigation and crop growth interact.

Problem context (community need): Irrigation systems often run during rain, wasting water. A cheap rain sensor in the field pauses irrigation as soon as it rains.

Design task: Design a physical, 3D-printable / laser-cut housing that holds the Arduino and integrates the Raindrop sensor, so a community can read and use the data.

User: farmers and school gardens.

Data Science Link

Your product exists to produce good data. This is how the unit's Four Big Ideas connect to the design.

What data does the sensor give? Measures raindrop levels using a 12-bit scale (0–100).

Low end: Lower values (analog): less water on the sensor, so a dry surface and no rain.
High end: Higher values (analog): more water detected, so rain present and the surface wet.

How the data is useful: Automatically pauses irrigation during rainfall, preventing overwatering.

1 · CollectionThe sensor reads the world; the housing must not disturb it.
2 · OrganisationArduino sends readings to Python; clean errors, missing values and duplicates.
3 · AnalysisLine, bar and pie charts reveal patterns and compare places or times.
4 · InterpretationThe community decides what to do with the pattern.

Modelling Worksheet — Making the Physical Model

Follow in order. Lab tools: 3D printer (PETG), laser cutter, hex driver, 320-grit sandpaper, hot glue gun.

  1. Check the CAD: open the chosen STL in TinkerCAD or your slicer, set units to millimetres and confirm the envelope is 124.0 × 124.0 × 141.7 mm (limit 140 × 140 × 160 mm).
  2. Slice the printed parts (Ground spike, Head housing, Head lid) in Cura/PrusaSlicer: PETG, 0.2 mm layers, 15–20% infill, supports only where an overhang needs them.
  3. Laser-cut the MDF parts (Ground flange) from 3 mm MDF using the top orthographic view as the footprint.
  4. De-burr and sand every printed part (320 grit); check the sensor opening and screw holes so nothing binds or blocks the sensor.
  5. Fit the sensor: Mount the rain plate on top of the stake with the traces facing up at about 15° tilt, and keep the control PCB under the lid.
  6. Assemble following the numbered exploded view and the BOM: fasteners finger-tight first.
  7. Wire and test: connect the sensor to the Arduino, upload your test sketch (Criterion C), and watch the readings in the Serial Monitor.
  8. Install in the community location from your design brief and confirm the sensor sees what it should.
  9. Function-test against specifications 7.1 and 7.2: Detects rain onset in under 5 seconds and pauses irrigation signalling within 10 seconds. Record the results in your portfolio.
  10. Finish: spray or vinyl-wrap in the colour scheme from specification 1.1, then re-test.
