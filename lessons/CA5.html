Design Brief

Context that frames the whole Criterion B response for this lesson.

Unit objective: Visualise data to determine the condition of the environment: toxic gases, emissions and air quality.

Problem context (community need): Traffic, construction dust and industrial emissions affect the air in many neighbourhoods. Poor air contributes to 1 in 9 deaths globally, yet most streets have no air-quality display.

Design task: Design a physical, 3D-printable / laser-cut housing that holds the Arduino and integrates the Gas (air quality) sensor, so a community can read and use the data.

User: residents, schools and environmental clubs.

Data Science Link

Your product exists to produce good data. This is how the unit's Four Big Ideas connect to the design.

What data does the sensor give? Measures harmful gas and particulate levels as an analog value.

Low end: Lower values: cleaner air.
High end: Higher values: more harmful gases or poor air quality.

How the data is useful: Systems can switch on air purifiers or alert citizens in real time.

1 · CollectionThe sensor reads the world; the housing must not disturb it.
2 · OrganisationArduino sends readings to Python; clean errors, missing values and duplicates.
3 · AnalysisLine, bar and pie charts reveal patterns and compare places or times.
4 · InterpretationThe community decides what to do with the pattern.

3.2Opens or unscrews with one tool so the Arduino and sensor can be re-wired and the USB cable reaches a laptop.Students need to re-flash the Arduino and re-collect data throughout the unit.
Environment4.1At least 80% of structural material is recyclable PETG or reclaimed MDF offcuts.PETG is recyclable and MDF offcuts come from the lab scrap bin (6 R's: Reduce, Reuse).
4.2Printed + cut mass ≤ 300 g.Lower mass means less filament, less print energy and an easier install.
Size5.1Overall envelope ≤ 150 × 150 × 160 mm (L × W × H).Small enough to fit on a bench, pole or desk and to be printed in sections.
5.2Every printed part fits a 200 × 200 mm print bed; every cut part fits a 300 × 300 mm laser bed.Matches the lab machines students will use.
Safety6.1Ventilated housing, no loose wires, and a fire-safe (self-extinguishing) filament near the heated sensor.Reduces the chance of injury or damage when students and the public use it.
6.2Low-voltage USB power only, no exposed sharp corners or live terminals.Required for any student-built electronic product.
Function7.1Refreshes an air-quality reading every 2 seconds and warms up in under 3 minutes.Directly measures whether the device does the job in the scenario.
7.2Mesh cap faces open air with at least 40% open area and nothing blocking it.The housing must help the sensor, not block or disturb it.
7.3The sensor's reading is available on the Arduino's serial port so Python can log, clean and graph it.This is how the device joins the Data Science workflow.
7.4Sensor can be swapped in under 2 minutes with hand tools.Sensors fail or need replacing in a busy classroom.
Material8.1PETG for housing and louvres (the gas sensor heats, so keep it vented); MDF for the base.Material is matched to where the device will live (sun, damp, skin contact, heat).
8.2Flat or simple parts use 3 mm laser-cut MDF; complex shapes are 3D printed.Uses each lab machine for what it does best.

Modelling Worksheet — Making the Physical Model

Follow in order. Lab tools: 3D printer (PETG), laser cutter, hex driver, 320-grit sandpaper, hot glue gun.

  1. Check the CAD: open the chosen STL in TinkerCAD or your slicer, set units to millimetres and confirm the envelope is 140.0 × 140.0 × 146.0 mm (limit 150 × 150 × 160 mm).
  2. Slice the printed parts (Corner post, Louvre roof, Louvre slat) in Cura/PrusaSlicer: PETG, 0.2 mm layers, 15–20% infill, supports only where an overhang needs them.
  3. Laser-cut the MDF parts (Base plate) from 3 mm MDF using the top orthographic view as the footprint.
  4. De-burr and sand every printed part (320 grit); check the sensor opening and screw holes so nothing binds or blocks the sensor.
  5. Fit the sensor: Seat the gas sensor can in the middle of the louvres with the mesh cap facing the airflow, and leave 10 mm of free air all round.
  6. Assemble following the numbered exploded view and the BOM: fasteners finger-tight first.
  7. Wire and test: connect the sensor to the Arduino, upload your test sketch (Criterion C), and watch the readings in the Serial Monitor.
  8. Install in the community location from your design brief and confirm the sensor sees what it should.
  9. Function-test against specifications 7.1 and 7.2: Refreshes an air-quality reading every 2 seconds and warms up in under 3 minutes. Record the results in your portfolio.
  10. Finish: spray or vinyl-wrap in the colour scheme from specification 1.1, then re-test.
