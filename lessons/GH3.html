Design Brief

Context that frames the whole Criterion B response for this lesson.

Unit objective: Visualise movement data to understand how physical activity affects health, prevent overexertion and encourage safe, ergonomic motion.

Problem context (community need): PE classes rarely measure how fast students move, so coaches can't spot fatigue or unsafe rushing. Portable timing gates would let any school track sprint speed and reaction time.

Design task: Design a physical, 3D-printable / laser-cut housing that holds the Arduino and integrates the Photogate sensor, so a community can read and use the data.

User: PE teachers, coaches and students.

Data Science Link

Your product exists to produce good data. This is how the unit's Four Big Ideas connect to the design.

What data does the sensor give? Measures the speed of a moving object or person, typically displayed in km/h.

Low end: Lower values: slower movement, calm and controlled actions.
High end: Higher values: faster movement, rapid or unsafe motion.

How the data is useful: Encourages safe and ergonomic motion, detects fatigue and improves reaction-time training.

1 · CollectionThe sensor reads the world; the housing must not disturb it.
2 · OrganisationArduino sends readings to Python; clean errors, missing values and duplicates.
3 · AnalysisLine, bar and pie charts reveal patterns and compare places or times.
4 · InterpretationThe community decides what to do with the pattern.

Modelling Worksheet — Making the Physical Model

Follow in order. Lab tools: 3D printer (PETG), laser cutter, hex driver, 320-grit sandpaper, hot glue gun.

  1. Check the CAD: open the chosen STL in TinkerCAD or your slicer, set units to millimetres and confirm the envelope is 150.0 × 140.0 × 149.0 mm (limit 160 × 150 × 160 mm).
  2. Slice the printed parts (Arch beam, Arch light, Arch pillar, Controller box) in Cura/PrusaSlicer: PETG, 0.2 mm layers, 15–20% infill, supports only where an overhang needs them.
  3. Laser-cut the MDF parts (Arch foot, Base plate) from 3 mm MDF using the top orthographic view as the footprint.
  4. De-burr and sand every printed part (320 grit); check the sensor opening and screw holes so nothing binds or blocks the sensor.
  5. Fit the sensor: Mount the photogate with its slot facing across the lane, emitter and receiver aligned within 2 mm, and fix the height with a stop screw.
  6. Assemble following the numbered exploded view and the BOM: fasteners finger-tight first.
  7. Wire and test: connect the sensor to the Arduino, upload your test sketch (Criterion C), and watch the readings in the Serial Monitor.
  8. Install in the community location from your design brief and confirm the sensor sees what it should.
  9. Function-test against specifications 7.1 and 7.2: Measures the speed of a person passing through the gate with a timing resolution of better than 10 ms. Record the results in your portfolio.
  10. Finish: spray or vinyl-wrap in the colour scheme from specification 1.1, then re-test.
