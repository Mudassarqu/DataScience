Design Brief

Context that frames the whole Criterion B response for this lesson.

Unit objective: Visualise sound-level patterns to understand how noise impacts health, reduce exposure to harmful noise and promote well-being.

Problem context (community need): Noisy canteens, corridors and libraries make it hard to concentrate and can harm hearing over time. A visible noise monitor would help classes keep to a healthy level.

Design task: Design a physical, 3D-printable / laser-cut housing that holds the Arduino and integrates the Sound sensor, so a community can read and use the data.

User: teachers, librarians and students.

Data Science Link

Your product exists to produce good data. This is how the unit's Four Big Ideas connect to the design.

What data does the sensor give? Measures sound intensity using analog or digital values, often scaled in decibels (dB).

Low end: Lower values: quieter environments, calm and restful spaces.
High end: Higher values: louder environments, possible stress, distraction or discomfort.

How the data is useful: Alerts or adjustments trigger when noise rises, supporting focus and reducing stress.

1 · CollectionThe sensor reads the world; the housing must not disturb it.
2 · OrganisationArduino sends readings to Python; clean errors, missing values and duplicates.
3 · AnalysisLine, bar and pie charts reveal patterns and compare places or times.
4 · InterpretationThe community decides what to do with the pattern.

Modelling Worksheet — Making the Physical Model

Follow in order. Lab tools: 3D printer (PETG), laser cutter, hex driver, 320-grit sandpaper, hot glue gun.

  1. Check the CAD: open the chosen STL in TinkerCAD or your slicer, set units to millimetres and confirm the envelope is 38.0 × 120.0 × 150.0 mm (limit 50 × 130 × 160 mm).
  2. Slice the printed parts (Display bezel, Display screen, Graph strip, Standoff) in Cura/PrusaSlicer: PETG, 0.2 mm layers, 15–20% infill, supports only where an overhang needs them.
  3. Laser-cut the MDF parts (Wall back plate) from 3 mm MDF using the top orthographic view as the footprint.
  4. De-burr and sand every printed part (320 grit); check the sensor opening and screw holes so nothing binds or blocks the sensor.
  5. Fit the sensor: Position the microphone can behind a 6 mm opening facing the room and add a small foam windscreen if the room has draughts.
  6. Assemble following the numbered exploded view and the BOM: fasteners finger-tight first.
  7. Wire and test: connect the sensor to the Arduino, upload your test sketch (Criterion C), and watch the readings in the Serial Monitor.
  8. Install in the community location from your design brief and confirm the sensor sees what it should.
  9. Function-test against specifications 7.1 and 7.2: Shows quiet / okay / loud status within 1 second of a change in noise. Record the results in your portfolio.
  10. Finish: spray or vinyl-wrap in the colour scheme from specification 1.1, then re-test.
