Design Brief

Context that frames the whole Criterion B response for this lesson.

Unit objective: Visualise flame sensor data to identify fire hazards or sudden flame presence in residential buildings, factories or public spaces.

Problem context (community need): Kitchens, electrical rooms and storage areas are common fire risks. A simple flame detector with a loud alarm can warn people seconds before smoke spreads.

Design task: Design a physical, 3D-printable / laser-cut housing that holds the Arduino and integrates the Flame (IR) sensor, so a community can read and use the data.

User: families, school labs and factories.

Data Science Link

Your product exists to produce good data. This is how the unit's Four Big Ideas connect to the design.

What data does the sensor give? Measures infrared (IR) radiation emitted from flames using analog or digital signals.

Low end: Low values: no flame detected, environment clear and safe.
High end: High values: flame or fire detected; may signal fire hazards or sparks.

How the data is useful: Triggers real-time alerts for homes, schools, labs and factories and supports quick response.

1 · CollectionThe sensor reads the world; the housing must not disturb it.
2 · OrganisationArduino sends readings to Python; clean errors, missing values and duplicates.
3 · AnalysisLine, bar and pie charts reveal patterns and compare places or times.
4 · InterpretationThe community decides what to do with the pattern.

Modelling Worksheet — Making the Physical Model

Follow in order. Lab tools: 3D printer (PETG), laser cutter, hex driver, 320-grit sandpaper, hot glue gun.

  1. Check the CAD: open the chosen STL in TinkerCAD or your slicer, set units to millimetres and confirm the envelope is 130.0 × 110.0 × 79.0 mm (limit 140 × 120 × 90 mm).
  2. Slice the printed parts (Alarm lamp, Housing, Visor hood) in Cura/PrusaSlicer: PETG, 0.2 mm layers, 15–20% infill, supports only where an overhang needs them.
  3. Laser-cut the MDF parts (Base plate) from 3 mm MDF using the top orthographic view as the footprint.
  4. De-burr and sand every printed part (320 grit); check the sensor opening and screw holes so nothing binds or blocks the sensor.
  5. Fit the sensor: Seat the IR receiver behind the hood opening, facing forward and slightly down, and keep the buzzer and lamp in the top of the housing.
  6. Assemble following the numbered exploded view and the BOM: fasteners finger-tight first.
  7. Wire and test: connect the sensor to the Arduino, upload your test sketch (Criterion C), and watch the readings in the Serial Monitor.
  8. Install in the community location from your design brief and confirm the sensor sees what it should.
  9. Function-test against specifications 7.1 and 7.2: Detects a lighter-sized flame within 1 m and sounds the alarm in under 2 seconds. Record the results in your portfolio.
  10. Finish: spray or vinyl-wrap in the colour scheme from specification 1.1, then re-test.
