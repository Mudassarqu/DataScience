Design Brief

Context that frames the whole Criterion B response for this lesson.

Unit objective: Visualise motion sensor data to identify movement patterns or unusual activity in hallways, public buildings, restricted areas or smart homes.

Problem context (community need): School corridors, labs and storerooms are unattended after hours, and automatic lights waste power when nobody is present. A motion sensor can alert staff and save electricity.

Design task: Design a physical, 3D-printable / laser-cut housing that holds the Arduino and integrates the Motion (PIR) sensor, so a community can read and use the data.

User: school security, building managers and families.

Data Science Link

Your product exists to produce good data. This is how the unit's Four Big Ideas connect to the design.

What data does the sensor give? Detects motion using infrared radiation, sending analog or digital signals.

Low end: Stable values: no motion detected, area secure and undisturbed.
High end: Spiked values: motion detected, possibly a person, object or intruder.

How the data is useful: Triggers real-time alerts in restricted areas and supports intrusion detection and crowd control.

1 · CollectionThe sensor reads the world; the housing must not disturb it.
2 · OrganisationArduino sends readings to Python; clean errors, missing values and duplicates.
3 · AnalysisLine, bar and pie charts reveal patterns and compare places or times.
4 · InterpretationThe community decides what to do with the pattern.

Modelling Worksheet — Making the Physical Model

Follow in order. Lab tools: 3D printer (PETG), laser cutter, hex driver, 320-grit sandpaper, hot glue gun.

  1. Check the CAD: open the chosen STL in TinkerCAD or your slicer, set units to millimetres and confirm the envelope is 91.0 × 60.0 × 74.0 mm (limit 110 × 70 × 90 mm).
  2. Slice the printed parts (Corner brace, Corner bracket, Sensor cradle, Swivel arm, Swivel ball) in Cura/PrusaSlicer: PETG, 0.2 mm layers, 15–20% infill, supports only where an overhang needs them.
  3. Laser-cut the MDF parts (Corner bracket base) from 3 mm MDF using the top orthographic view as the footprint.
  4. De-burr and sand every printed part (320 grit); check the sensor opening and screw holes so nothing binds or blocks the sensor.
  5. Fit the sensor: Seat the PIR lens dome through the front window with the lens clear of the housing, and tighten the ball joint only after aiming.
  6. Assemble following the numbered exploded view and the BOM: fasteners finger-tight first.
  7. Wire and test: connect the sensor to the Arduino, upload your test sketch (Criterion C), and watch the readings in the Serial Monitor.
  8. Install in the community location from your design brief and confirm the sensor sees what it should.
  9. Function-test against specifications 7.1 and 7.2: Detects a person walking at 1 m/s within a 4 m range and reports within 1 second. Record the results in your portfolio.
  10. Finish: spray or vinyl-wrap in the colour scheme from specification 1.1, then re-test.
