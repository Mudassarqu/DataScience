Design Brief

Context that frames the whole Criterion B response for this lesson.

Unit objective: Visualise environmental and health data to detect harmful conditions, support early health alerts and promote safety.

Problem context (community need): Cooking fumes, cleaning products and poor ventilation can make indoor air unhealthy without anyone noticing, especially in small flats and classrooms.

Design task: Design a physical, 3D-printable / laser-cut housing that holds the Arduino and integrates the Gas sensor, so a community can read and use the data.

User: families, schools and care homes.

Data Science Link

Your product exists to produce good data. This is how the unit's Four Big Ideas connect to the design.

What data does the sensor give? Measures gas levels in the air as an analog value.

Low end: Lower values: cleaner air.
High end: Higher values: poorer air quality or harmful gases present.

How the data is useful: Fans or purifiers can switch on automatically and alerts protect children and the elderly.

1 · CollectionThe sensor reads the world; the housing must not disturb it.
2 · OrganisationArduino sends readings to Python; clean errors, missing values and duplicates.
3 · AnalysisLine, bar and pie charts reveal patterns and compare places or times.
4 · InterpretationThe community decides what to do with the pattern.

Safety6.1Vented housing, no heat near plastic, and a clear audible alert for dangerous gas levels.Reduces the chance of injury or damage when students and the public use it.

Modelling Worksheet — Making the Physical Model

Follow in order. Lab tools: 3D printer (PETG), laser cutter, hex driver, 320-grit sandpaper, hot glue gun.

  1. Check the CAD: open the chosen STL in TinkerCAD or your slicer, set units to millimetres and confirm the envelope is 140.0 × 110.0 × 62.5 mm (limit 150 × 120 × 80 mm).
  2. Slice the printed parts (Pod body, Pod top lip, Sensor plinth) in Cura/PrusaSlicer: PETG, 0.2 mm layers, 15–20% infill, supports only where an overhang needs them.
  3. Laser-cut the MDF parts (Base plate) from 3 mm MDF using the top orthographic view as the footprint.
  4. De-burr and sand every printed part (320 grit); check the sensor opening and screw holes so nothing binds or blocks the sensor.
  5. Fit the sensor: Fit the gas sensor can facing up through the pod top with the mesh cap exposed, and keep any hot components 15 mm away from plastic.
  6. Assemble following the numbered exploded view and the BOM: fasteners finger-tight first.
  7. Wire and test: connect the sensor to the Arduino, upload your test sketch (Criterion C), and watch the readings in the Serial Monitor.
  8. Install in the community location from your design brief and confirm the sensor sees what it should.
  9. Function-test against specifications 7.1 and 7.2: Shows good / caution / poor air status within 5 seconds of a change. Record the results in your portfolio.
  10. Finish: spray or vinyl-wrap in the colour scheme from specification 1.1, then re-test.
