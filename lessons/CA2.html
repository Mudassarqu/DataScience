Design Brief

Context that frames the whole Criterion B response for this lesson.

Unit objective: Visualise rainfall data to monitor weather patterns, support early warning systems and strengthen climate resilience.

Problem context (community need): Flash rain on dry UAE roads causes flooding and accidents within minutes, but most streets have no rain alert. A cheap rain post could warn drivers and pause irrigation straight away.

Design task: Design a physical, 3D-printable / laser-cut housing that holds the Arduino and integrates the Raindrop sensor, so a community can read and use the data.

User: a municipality, a school or a neighbourhood watering its gardens.

Data Science Link

Your product exists to produce good data. This is how the unit's Four Big Ideas connect to the design.

What data does the sensor give? Measures raindrop level on a 12-bit scale shown as 0–100.

Low end: Lower values: less water on the sensor, so a dry surface and no rain.
High end: Higher values: more water detected, so rain is present and the surface is wet.

How the data is useful: Automatically pauses irrigation during rainfall, reducing water waste.

1 · CollectionThe sensor reads the world; the housing must not disturb it.
2 · OrganisationArduino sends readings to Python; clean errors, missing values and duplicates.
3 · AnalysisLine, bar and pie charts reveal patterns and compare places or times.
4 · InterpretationThe community decides what to do with the pattern.

Modelling Worksheet — Making the Physical Model

Follow in order. Lab tools: 3D printer (PETG), laser cutter, hex driver, 320-grit sandpaper, hot glue gun.

  1. Check the CAD: open the chosen STL in TinkerCAD or your slicer, set units to millimetres and confirm the envelope is 154.0 × 112.0 × 130.0 mm (limit 170 × 130 × 140 mm).
  2. Slice the printed parts (Collector scale, Collector tube, Funnel leg, Rain funnel, Sensor bracket) in Cura/PrusaSlicer: PETG, 0.2 mm layers, 15–20% infill, supports only where an overhang needs them.
  3. Laser-cut the MDF parts (Base plate) from 3 mm MDF using the top orthographic view as the footprint.
  4. De-burr and sand every printed part (320 grit); check the sensor opening and screw holes so nothing binds or blocks the sensor.
  5. Fit the sensor: Mount the rain plate on its bracket with the copper traces facing up, tilted about 15°, and keep the control PCB under cover.
  6. Assemble following the numbered exploded view and the BOM: fasteners finger-tight first.
  7. Wire and test: connect the sensor to the Arduino, upload your test sketch (Criterion C), and watch the readings in the Serial Monitor.
  8. Install in the community location from your design brief and confirm the sensor sees what it should.
  9. Function-test against specifications 7.1 and 7.2: Detects the start of rain within 5 seconds and reports a 0–100 rain level. Record the results in your portfolio.
  10. Finish: spray or vinyl-wrap in the colour scheme from specification 1.1, then re-test.
