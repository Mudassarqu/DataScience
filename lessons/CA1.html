Design Brief

Context that frames the whole Criterion B response for this lesson.

Unit objective: Visualise humidity and temperature to monitor weather patterns, support early warning systems and strengthen climate resilience.

Problem context (community need): Dubai's summer humidity can jump from 30% to 90% in a day, and neighbourhood weather stations are few. A low-cost community weather post would let schools and residents see heat and humidity warnings for their own street.

Design task: Design a physical, 3D-printable / laser-cut housing that holds the Arduino and integrates the Moisture sensor (humidity % and temperature °C), so a community can read and use the data.

User: a school eco-club or residents' association measuring street-level weather.

Data Science Link

Your product exists to produce good data. This is how the unit's Four Big Ideas connect to the design.

What data does the sensor give? Humidity is measured in %, temperature in °C or °F.

Low end: Lower values mean less moisture in the air: dry weather.
High end: Higher values mean more moisture in the air: humid weather.

How the data is useful: Systems can adjust automatically to humidity and temperature, saving energy and water.

1 · CollectionThe sensor reads the world; the housing must not disturb it.
2 · OrganisationArduino sends readings to Python; clean errors, missing values and duplicates.
3 · AnalysisLine, bar and pie charts reveal patterns and compare places or times.
4 · InterpretationThe community decides what to do with the pattern.

B2 · Design Ideas 3 different ideas

Three ideas that differ in form and mechanism, not just looks. Each sketch is an isometric render of the 3D CAD model with the sensor and base labelled.

Concept 1 — “Louvre Weather Tower” SELECTED

Stevenson-screen louvre stack

Six angled louvres and a leaf-green roof shade the humidity sensor while air passes through, like a real weather screen. Low cost and very accurate.

Envelope 140.0 × 140.0 × 146.0 mm · ≈ 187 g · ≈ AED 46

Concept 2 — “Climate Wall Panel”

Wall display with live graph

A wall panel shows the humidity and temperature on a screen with a status light bar. Easy to read, but the wall can warm the sensor.

Envelope 39.6 × 120.0 × 150.0 mm · ≈ 67 g · ≈ AED 32

Concept 3 — “Cloud Dome”

Spherical sky-facing housing

A sphere on a pedestal houses the electronics with the sensor at the top. Eye-catching, but the dome traps heat.

Envelope 120.0 × 120.0 × 125.6 mm · ≈ 274 g · ≈ AED 55

Modelling Worksheet — Making the Physical Model

Follow in order. Lab tools: 3D printer (PETG), laser cutter, hex driver, 320-grit sandpaper, hot glue gun.

  1. Check the CAD: open the chosen STL in TinkerCAD or your slicer, set units to millimetres and confirm the envelope is 140.0 × 140.0 × 146.0 mm (limit 150 × 150 × 160 mm).
  2. Slice the printed parts (Corner post, Louvre roof, Louvre slat) in Cura/PrusaSlicer: PETG, 0.2 mm layers, 15–20% infill, supports only where an overhang needs them.
  3. Laser-cut the MDF parts (Base plate) from 3 mm MDF using the top orthographic view as the footprint.
  4. De-burr and sand every printed part (320 grit); check the sensor opening and screw holes so nothing binds or blocks the sensor.
  5. Fit the sensor: Fix the humidity sensor behind the louvres with its slits facing the airflow, at least 10 mm from any wall or hot electronics.
  6. Assemble following the numbered exploded view and the BOM: fasteners finger-tight first.
  7. Wire and test: connect the sensor to the Arduino, upload your test sketch (Criterion C), and watch the readings in the Serial Monitor.
  8. Install in the community location from your design brief and confirm the sensor sees what it should.
  9. Function-test against specifications 7.1 and 7.2: Reads humidity (0–100%) and temperature every 5 seconds without the housing trapping heat (inside no more than 1 °C above outside). Record the results in your portfolio.
  10. Finish: spray or vinyl-wrap in the colour scheme from specification 1.1, then re-test.
